# Specifying the target system

> [!QUESTION] Goal: what must be fixed to specify the system that an inquiry is about?

The target system is the first component of the [subject of inquiry](subject-of-inquiry.md).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A target system is a part of the world that the modeller carves out by partitioning and abstraction. Specifying it is a modelling step in its own right [elliottgraves2020].

- **The specifications are admitted as given.** The laws and properties recorded about the system are those admitted *a priori* as relevant to characterize it. The inquiry may still seek new ones. Formal and empirical targets differ only in the closure of those laws. For a formal target, the laws define the system, so a new law must be derivable from them, for instance an effective equation obtained in a mean-field limit. For an empirical target, a new law may be independent of the ones admitted, for instance a newly discovered plasticity mechanism.  *Example*: the Edwards–Anderson spin glass consists of $N$ Ising spins $s_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ on the sites of a lattice, interacting through random couplings $J_{ij}$ between neighbouring sites, with energy $H=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}s_is_j$. The state space is $\{-1,+1\}^N$; the site positions and the couplings are parameters, fixed while the spins evolve [edwards1975]. ^constituents
	
- **For the target type, the option "No external target" covers questions about a model class itself**, such as its own [consequences, regimes, limits, structural properties](epistemic-aim.md).
	
- **The reference class is generated by the other specifications.** Its members are the systems that satisfy the constituents, the variables and the laws, with parameters in their admitted value sets as restricted by $D$. For a particular system, the class reduces to that system. The members of the reference class are the instances indexed by $k$ in the phenomenon.

> [!IMPORTANT] No redundancy
> The reference class records only a name and, when needed, selection criteria. It does not restate any of the other specifications. Together they form a schema, and each member of the class is one instance of that schema, with its own parameter values, as a theory specifies a family of models [winther2020]. 

- **Selection criteria are needed whenever membership depends on a property that the other specifications do not fix**, for a formal class as for an empirical one. *Examples*: species or brain area for recorded animals, as eligibility criteria define the population of a clinical trial [ich2019]; convergence of training for trained networks. 

- **A criterion on an outcome of the dynamics can create associations that the full class does not show.** *Example*: across the full class, the learning rate and the initialization scale are set independently, but training converges only when their product stays below a threshold. Among the converged networks, a large learning rate then co-occurs with a small initialization scale. This association comes from the selection alone, since convergence is a common effect of both factors [hernan2004]. Therefore, each selection criterion is marked as fixed *a priori* or dependent on an outcome. A claim about members selected on an outcome does not transfer to the full class without a check for this bias.

- **State variables fix how the phenomena can be expressed.** The explanandum variable of a phenomenon is a *function of the system's state variables* (an "observable"). A variable defined over one constituent, or over a pair of them, corresponds to the [components and relations of a mechanism](mechanism.md).

- **A parameter or a variable names the constituents it is defined over**, one entry per place: a quantity carried by a single unit names one kind of constituent, a quantity carried by a pair names two, and a quantity of the system as a whole names none. The places are generic, so a connectivity weight defined over two neurons holds for any pair of them. A quantity whose single value holds for every tuple is marked as shared. An aggregate of constituent states is stated with its defining map, "[variable] := [function of constituent variables]". ^places
	
- **An aggregate and a quantity of the whole system differ by derivability.** An aggregate is a function of constituent states, so aggregates are functions on the state space, not additional coordinates. *Examples*: the center of mass is a mass-weighted mean of positions, the temperature of a gas a mean kinetic energy, a population rate a mean over neurons. In contrast, a quantity of the whole system cannot be derived from constituent states. *Example*: the volume of a container. ^aggregate-variables
	
- **An aggregation map is a definition, so it is stated with its variable, not among the laws.** An aggregation map merely *introduces a variable*, so it cannot be false of the system. *Example*: the map "population rate := mean of the constituent rates". In contrast, a bridge law *identifies a separately measured quantity with an aggregate*, so it is a factual hypothesis. *Example*: the temperature read by a thermometer equals the mean kinetic energy of the molecules, up to a constant. In reduction theory, the status of such bridge laws is either stipulation or empirical hypothesis [nagel1961]. A bridge law that states an empirical hypothesis belongs to the laws, under the measurement function.

- **Internal variables are the state variables of the system $S$**: their values are determined within $S$, as the values of endogenous variables are determined by variables inside a causal model [pearl2009]. The determining law need not be known: an internal variable may be only measured, and its law may be admitted, sought by the inquiry, or left open. 

- **External systems enter the specification only through the variables they share with the target system $S$.** An open system interacts with its environment through variables shared at its boundary [willems2007]. The external systems are therefore specified by the properties that constrain those shared variables. These properties pass the [inclusion test](subject-of-inquiry.md#^inclusion-test): an answer may explain the phenomenon by the structure of the inputs, for instance by the latent variables that generate them [goldt2020], or by the class of external system that reads the outputs, for instance a linear decoder. A restriction of their parameter ranges belongs to $D$, as for the parameters of $S$.

- **Boundary between internal and external variables.** The interface lists only the variables shared across the partition between interior and exterior, with their direction. The labels "input" and "output" come from systems theory, where they apply to any system. When shared variables cannot be split by direction, as in a closed loop, the coupling option records these variables. The behavioral approach to systems treats every interconnection as a set of shared variables, without a prior partition into inputs and outputs [willems2007].
	
- **The partition of variables is only provisional.** The constituents, the state variables and the interface record the partition that the researcher *stipulates*, for example an isolated slice or a closed loop with a task environment. In some contexts, the relevant partition is itself partly a *result of the inquiry*. *Example*: in [constitutive explanation](requested-relation.md), a part belongs to the mechanism only if its activity and the system's behavior can each be changed by intervening on the other ("mutual manipulability") [craver2007]. The inquiry may then move a variable across the interface, for instance by enlarging $S$ to include the task environment.
