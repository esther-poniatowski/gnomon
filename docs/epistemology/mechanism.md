# Mechanism — Definition

> [!QUESTION] Goal: what are the constituents of a mechanism?
 
> [!IMPORTANT] Mechanistic modeling
> A mechanistic explanation states that a phenomenon is **generated by organized interactions of specific components**.
> This claim asserts more than a correlation between certain variables and the phenomenon.

## Constituents of a mechanism

A mechanism is specified through four aspects [machamer2000] [bechtel2005]:

| Canonical aspect | Common synonyms                                               | Question                                                                                                   |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Phenomenon**   | explanandum                                                   | What must be explained?                                                                                    |
| **Components**   | parts, entities, objects                                      | Which relevant parts of the system, and which of their properties, contribute to the phenomenon?           |
| **Activities**   | operations, interactions, processes                           | What transformations or interactions occur between components?                                             |
| **Organization** | arrangement, configuration, structure of parts and activities | How are components and activities arranged so that their effects combine to produce the target phenomenon? |

## Definitions

| Term                                               | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                | Characterized by                                                                                                                                                       | Examples                                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Phenomenon**                                     | the explanandum: the behavior, process, capacity, event, or regularity of the system to account for                                                                                                       | inferred or detected from data                                                                                                                                         | - action potential<br>- DNA replication<br>- collective beliefs                                                                  |
| **Components**                                     | a part of the system treated as an *individuated object* (relative to the chosen level of description), bearing properties and causal capacities<br>                                                      | - a state space<br>- intrinsic parameters<br>- a state (indexed by time)                                                                                                  | - a neuron in a circuit<br>- a protein in a signaling pathway<br>- an agent in a social network                                  |
| **Activity**                                       | a *process* through which entities contribute to the phenomenon or interact                                                                                                                               | unary, binary, or of arbitrary arity [illari2012]                                                                                                                                   | - phosphorylating<br>- firing<br>- transporting<br>- exerting force<br>- exchanging information                                  |
| **Organization**                                   | a *causal architecture* that produces the phenomenon: a higher-order arrangement of components and activities that combines the causal capacities of the parts into one capacity of the whole system | constraints on how entities and activities must be instantiated to realize the phenomenon (e.g. sequencing, spatial position, temporal order, hierarchical composition) | - modularity<br>- feedback loops<br>- synchronization<br>- conditional activation<br>- routing of signals<br>- division of labor |
| **Structure** (if distinguished from organization) | a stable *pattern of components and relations* constituting the system                                                                                                                                    | a graph where vertices correspond to components and edges to relations, with a specific topology or geometry                                                           | - a correlation matrix<br>- a hierarchy<br>- a lattice <br>- a social network                                                    |
| **Relation** (if distinguished from activity)      | how two or more components stand with respect to one another                                                                                                                                              | - two (or more) components<br>- additional properties (e.g. direction, strength, delay, sign, spatial geometry, conditionality)                                        | - a neuron synapses onto another<br>- a protein binds another<br>- an agent communicates with another                            |

## Clarifications

**A phenomenon is not identical to its raw data**. Phenomena (e.g. processes, regularities, effects) are *inferred* or *detected* from data, and then explained by theories or mechanisms [bogen1988] [woodward2000] [mattig2025]. A phenomenon may also be a singular or irregular occurrence, so the definition does not require repeatability [craver2024].

**Componenthood is relational and depends on the scale of description**. A component may itself contain internal structure. *Example*: a neuron is an entity at one explanatory level and an organized system of entities at another.

**Activity is local, whereas organization is global**. An activity is a *local process or transformation*. Organization coordinates *multiple entities and activities* by arranging their local transformations relative to one another. *Example*: the statement "$c_1$ inhibits $c_2$" describes an activity or interaction. The extended statement "$c_1$ inhibits $c_2$ after $c_3$ activates $c_1$, forming a delayed negative-feedback loop" describes organization.

**Dynamics is one possible realization of causal structure**. Dynamics specifies how the states of a system evolve, typically through differential equations. Causal dependencies, however, need not constitute a temporal dynamical law, and not all mechanistic descriptions admit local ODE representations. Causal structure may instead specify *which variables, when changed, would make a difference to which others*, typically through dependencies that support interventions or counterfactuals. A mechanistic account may therefore explicitly state, beyond the behavior of its components, a causal dependence that stays invariant under intervention [woodward2003].

**Organization is a higher-order property**. An organization is a *non-aggregative* property of multiple components and activities. The phenomenon depends not only on the parts and activities present but on their *arrangement* and relations. An ordinary binary graph therefore does not in general represent an organization. A *typed relational structure* admits constraints and relations of arity greater than two, and therefore represents an organization more adequately. Such a structure can encode the spatial, temporal, causal, hierarchical and topological relations among parts and activities simultaneously. *Examples*: a correlation matrix describes structure even when no causal interpretation has yet been supplied. Two neural systems can share one graph topology and still have different dynamics, because their synaptic signs, transfer functions or intrinsic neuronal properties diverge.

**A relation is not necessarily causal**. *Examples*: the relations "activates" and "inhibits" are causal, but "is spatially adjacent to" and "has the same value as" are not. In a mechanism, a causal relation is often characterized by counterfactual dependence under interventions on the relevant variables (interventionist accounts [woodward2003]).

**Mechanistic explanation is selective, relative to a phenomenon**. A mechanistic representation selects or abstracts the *subset* of components and activities that is causally relevant to explain the target phenomenon. The representation neither describes the microstructure of the system exhaustively nor reproduces reality. An exhaustive description may leave implicit the organizational properties that matter for the phenomenon. *Example*: a neural network can be described by a full connectivity matrix (a complete anatomical graph). Yet only some of that structure may matter for a particular behavior (e.g. modularity, hierarchy, motifs, existence of a feedback cycle, degeneracy, redundancy, bottlenecks, multiscale coupling).

## Formalism (proposed)

> [!WARNING] No canonical mathematical notation exists in the philosophical literature. The formalism therefore takes its notation from the [registry shared across the framework](notation.md), as does the [form of an answer](answer-form.md).

**Mechanism**. A generic mechanistic schema is a triple
$$\langle C,L,O\rangle$$
where:
- $C$: components, also called entities or parts;
- $L$: activities or interaction rules;
- $O$: organization of those components and activities.

**Components**: a set of objects
$$C=\{c_1,\dots,c_n\}$$
where each component $c_i$ has a state space $\mathcal{X}_i$, parameters $\theta_i$, and a state:
$$x_i(t)\in \mathcal{X}_i$$

**Activity**: often a dynamical rule that fixes how the state of one component changes in response to the states of the others. For each component $c_i$:
$$\frac{dx_i}{dt} = F_i \left( x_i, \{x_j:j\in \mathcal{N}_i\}, \theta \right)$$
where:
- $x_i$ is the state of the component;
- $\mathcal{N}_i$ collects the components that influence $c_i$ (read from the structure);
- $F_i$ specifies a local operation or interaction rule.

**Organization**: a structure over components and activities, written as a family of typed relations:
$$O=\{\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_m\}, \qquad \rho_k\subseteq U^{n_k}, \qquad U = C \sqcup L$$
where each relation $\rho_k$ encodes an organizational constraint.
*Example*: the relations
$$\rho_{\mathrm{part}}\subseteq C\times L,\qquad \rho_{\mathrm{temp}}\subseteq L\times L,\qquad \rho_{\mathrm{causal}}\subseteq L\times L$$
may respectively encode:

- the components that perform each activity;
- the temporal precedence among activities;
- the causal influence among activities.

**Relation**: a family of subsets indexed by arity, in which the member $\rho_k$ selects $k$-tuples of components:
$$\rho=\{\rho_k\}_{k\ge1}, \qquad \rho_k\subseteq C^k$$
*Examples*: A binary relation may be written:
$$r_{ij} = (c_i,c_j)\in \rho \qquad \text{or} \qquad r_{ij}=(i,j,w_{ij},\delta_{ij},\ldots)$$
where the second form represents richer relations that include additional properties (e.g. weight, delay).

*Examples*: relations can encode productive constraints (e.g. spatial adjacency, temporal precedence, causal dependence, participation of a component in an activity, hierarchical containment):

- $c_i\prec c_j$ may encode temporal precedence;
- $d(c_i,c_j)<\varepsilon$ may encode a spatial constraint;
- $\ell_i\rightarrow \ell_j$ may encode a causal ordering, where $L=\{\ell_1,\dots,\ell_m\}$.

**Phenomenon**. A phenomenon is often represented as a predicate on the trajectories of the system:
$$P[x(\cdot)]$$
or as an input-output behavior:
$$P:\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{in}}\rightarrow \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{out}}$$
*Example*: a population oscillation can be characterized by a property of the trajectory $y(t)$ of the aggregate activity.

**Mechanism**. A mechanism produces a phenomenon $P$:
$$\langle C,L,O\rangle\models P$$
or, more explicitly, with a realization map:
$$\Phi(C,L,O)=P$$
*Example*: in a neural mechanism,

- the phenomenon is a population oscillation;
- the components are neurons;
- the activities are the dynamics of excitation, inhibition, and adaptation;
- the structure is the complete directed synaptic connectivity (e.g. signs, weights, delays, geometry);
- the organization identifies a mechanistically salient pattern, possibly already encoded in the detailed connectivity and dynamics (e.g. $\text{E}\rightarrow\text{I}\dashv\text{E}$ may be described organizationally as a negative-feedback loop between excitation and inhibition).
