# Specifying a mechanistic answer

> [!QUESTION] Goal: which aspects must a mechanistic answer state, and in which formalism?

> [!IMPORTANT] Mechanistic modeling
> A mechanistic explanation states that a phenomenon is **generated by organized activities of specific components**.
> This claim asserts more than a correlation between certain variables and the phenomenon.

The senses of mechanism and the test of a mechanistic model are stated in the [vocabulary of mechanism](definitions/mechanism-and-mechanistic-model.md). Each aspect of a mechanistic answer is defined in the note named in the column *Defined in*. For each aspect, this note fixes the content required of a mechanistic answer and the formalism of that content.

## Aspects of a mechanistic answer

A mechanistic answer states four aspects [machamer2000] [bechtel2005]:

| Canonical aspect | Common synonyms          | Question                                                                                                   | Defined in |
| ---------------- | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| **Phenomenon**   | explanandum              | What must be explained?                                                                                    | [pattern and phenomenon](definitions/pattern-and-phenomenon.md) |
| **Components**   | parts, entities, objects | Which relevant parts of the system, and which of their properties, contribute to the phenomenon?           | [system, constituent and environment](definitions/system-constituent-environment.md) |
| **Activities**   | operations               | Which changes do the components produce, alone or by acting on other components?                           | [activity](definitions/activity.md) |
| **Organization** | arrangement              | How are components and activities arranged so that their effects combine to produce the target phenomenon? | [system, constituent and environment](definitions/system-constituent-environment.md) |

## Requirements on the answer

- **The answer has mechanistic form, and passes the explanatory mapping when the question requests an actual explanation.** Both tests are stated for a [mechanistic model](definitions/mechanism-and-mechanistic-model.md#^mechanistic-model-test). Therefore, each component states its map from the declared constituents.
- **Each activity is stated as an action or as an interaction.** The statement "$c_1$ inhibits $c_2$" states an *action* of $c_1$ on $c_2$. The same pair forms an *interaction* only when $c_2$ also acts on $c_1$, by the [distinction between action and interaction](definitions/interaction-and-cause.md#^action-interaction).
- **The organization is stated apart from the activities.** The statement "$c_1$ inhibits $c_2$ after $c_2$ activates $c_1$, forming a delayed negative-feedback loop" states an organization, because it arranges two activities relative to each other. Each of the two activities is local, whereas the loop is a property of their arrangement, as the [vocabulary of activity](definitions/activity.md) separates them.
- **The answer may state its causal structure without a law of evolution.** A causal structure [need not be a dynamic](definitions/dynamic.md#^causal-structure), so the laws $L$ that state the activities of a mechanistic answer may be dependencies that support interventions.

## Formalism (proposed)

> [!WARNING] No canonical mathematical notation exists in the philosophical literature. Therefore, the formalism takes its notation from the [registry shared across the framework](notation.md), as does the [form of an answer](answer-form.md).

**Mechanism**. A generic mechanistic schema is a triple
$$\langle C,L,O\rangle$$
where:
- $C$: components, also called entities or parts;
- $L$: activities, each an action or an interaction;
- $O$: organization of those components and activities.

**Components**: a set of objects
$$C=\{c_1,\dots,c_n\}$$
where each component $c_i$ has a state space $\mathcal{X}_i$, parameters $\theta_i$, and a state:
$$x_i(t)\in \mathcal{X}_i$$

**Activity**: often one term of a dynamical rule. The rule of a component $c_i$ fixes how its state changes in response to the states of the others. This rule sums the activities that change the state of $c_i$:
$$\frac{dx_i}{dt} = F_i \left( x_i, \{x_j:j\in \mathcal{N}_i\}, \theta \right) = \sum_{\ell\in L_i} f_\ell \left( x_i, \{x_j:j\in \mathcal{N}_i\}, \theta \right)$$
where:
- $x_i$ is the state of the component;
- $\mathcal{N}_i$ collects the components that act on $c_i$ (read from the structure);
- $F_i$ is the rule of the component;
- $L_i\subseteq L$ collects the activities that change the state of $c_i$;
- each term $f_\ell$ is one activity, in the sense of the [criterion of an activity term](definitions/activity.md#^activity-term), and specifies one local operation or interaction rule.

**Organization**: a structure over components and activities, written as a family of typed relations:
$$O=\{\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_m\}, \qquad \rho_k\subseteq U^{n_k}, \qquad U = C \sqcup L$$
where each relation $\rho_k$ encodes an organizational constraint.
*Example*: the relations
$$\rho_{\mathrm{part}}\subseteq C\times L,\qquad \rho_{\mathrm{temp}}\subseteq L\times L,\qquad \rho_{\mathrm{causal}}\subseteq L\times L$$
may respectively encode:

- the components that perform each activity;
- the temporal precedence among activities;
- the causal influence among activities.

A typed relational structure is required because an ordinary graph with binary edges does not in general represent an organization. An organization is a non-aggregative property of several components and activities, and some of its relations have three or more terms. *Example*: two neural systems can share one graph topology and still have different dynamics, because their synaptic signs, transfer functions or intrinsic neuronal properties differ.

**Relation**: a family of subsets indexed by arity, in which the member $\rho^{(a)}$ selects $a$-tuples of components:
$$\rho=\{\rho^{(a)}\}_{a\ge1}, \qquad \rho^{(a)}\subseteq C^a$$
*Example*: a binary relation may be written:
$$r_{ij} = (c_i,c_j)\in \rho^{(2)} \qquad \text{or} \qquad r_{ij}=(i,j,w_{ij},\delta_{ij},\ldots)$$
where the second form represents richer relations that include additional properties (e.g. weight, delay).

*Examples*: relations can encode productive constraints (e.g. spatial adjacency, temporal precedence, causal dependence, participation of a component in an activity, hierarchical containment):

- $c_i\prec c_j$ may encode temporal precedence;
- $d(c_i,c_j)<\varepsilon$ may encode a spatial constraint;
- $\ell_i\rightarrow \ell_j$ may encode a causal ordering, where $L=\{\ell_1,\dots,\ell_m\}$.

**Phenomenon**. A phenomenon is often represented as a predicate on the trajectories of the system, classified by the occurrence classes of the [vocabulary of process](definitions/process-event-history.md):
$$P[x(\cdot)]$$
or as an input–output behavior, a [function computed](definitions/computation.md) by the system:
$$P:\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{in}}\rightarrow \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{out}}$$
*Example*: a population oscillation can be characterized by a property of the trajectory $y(t)$ of the aggregate activity.

**Mechanism**. A mechanism produces a phenomenon $P$:
$$\langle C,L,O\rangle\models P$$
or, more explicitly, with a realization map:
$$\Phi(C,L,O)=P$$
*Example*: in a neural mechanism,

- the phenomenon is a population oscillation;
- the components are neurons;
- the activities are the dynamics of excitation, inhibition, and adaptation;
- the structure is the complete directed synaptic connectivity (e.g. signs, weights, delays, geometry);
- the organization identifies a mechanistically salient pattern, possibly already encoded in the detailed connectivity and dynamics (e.g. $\text{E}\rightarrow\text{I}\dashv\text{E}$ may be described organizationally as a negative-feedback loop between excitation and inhibition).
