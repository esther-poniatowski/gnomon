## Overlaps and distinctions

### Relevance

Relevance has several distinct meanings:

- **logical/inferential relevance**: the premise is actually used
- **probabilistic relevance**: the premise changes credence
- **explanatory relevance**: the factor makes a difference to the phenomenon
- **dialectical relevance**: the move contributes to resolving the dispute

Relevance can be assessed at distinct granularity levels in a reasoning:

- **Scope admissibility** is project-level and external to the note.
- **Objective unity** governs whether a note answers one inferential objective or several.
- **Local necessity** governs whether a given unit is needed within a note, once the objective is fixed.

This prevents a frequent failure mode in agent systems: a note that is locally coherent but globally off-mission.

### Transparency, Explanation, Understanding

**Transparency**, **Explanation**, and **Understanding** are distinct. A discourse may be:

- **transparent but non-explanatory**
- **explanatory but still opaque**
- **transparent and explanatory but still not understanding-producing**

### Sufficiency, Robustness

**Sufficiency** and **Robustness** are distinct. A set of premises may be sufficient in a narrow sense and yet:

- fragile under prior variation
- defeated by correlated evidence
- unable to answer objections
- unusable for transfer

### Unification, Economy

**Unification** and **Economy** are related but not equivalent:

- **economy** reduces unnecessary load
- **unification** reveals that many cases instantiate one deeper structure

### Abstraction levels

Appropriate abstraction is a balance principle. This principle is irreducible. It mediates between two symmetrical failures:

- too much low-level detail, producing overload
- too much abstraction, erasing the real dependency structure

### Premise legitimacy, Proof strategy

Genuine distinction:

- **Premise legitimacy** asks whether the derivation is allowed to use a premise at all.
- **Proof strategy** asks how the derivation is routed and what explanatory mechanism it must reveal.

A proof can be perfectly legitimate in premises and still be poor in structure; conversely, a strategically elegant proof can still hide illicit assumptions.
